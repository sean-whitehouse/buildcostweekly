Change Orders: Pricing Them Fairly and Fast

Guides › Estimating · Evergreen — reviewed September 2026

Price a change order the same way you priced the original bid — materials, labor, equipment or disposal, then overhead and margin on top — and add any schedule impact separately. Get it in writing and signed before the work happens, not at the end of the job. The two things that cost small operators money are pricing changes at cost because the request felt small, and doing the work first and asking for the money later. A change order is a small contract, and it should take you about five minutes to write.

Nearly every remodel and small residential job changes after the contract is signed. That is normal and not a sign anything went wrong. What separates a job that stays profitable from one that quietly bleeds is not whether changes happen — it is whether each one gets priced and papered on the day it comes up.

What counts as a change order

Anything that alters the scope, the specification, or the schedule from what the signed contract describes. The common ones on small jobs:

  • Owner-requested additions. "While you're in there, can you also…" — the single most expensive sentence on any remodel.
  • Substitutions. A different tile, a different window, a different grade of lumber. Even a same-price substitution can carry a lead time or a labor difference.
  • Unforeseen conditions. Rot behind the sheathing, undersized wiring, a slab that isn't level. You did not cause it and you did not price it.
  • Design or plan revisions. Handed down from the owner, architect, or building department mid-job.
  • Schedule changes you don't control. An owner who delays a selection, or another trade running late, can push your crew into a second mobilization.

Deletions are change orders too, and they should get the same paperwork. A credit that never gets written down becomes an argument at the final invoice.

The rule that saves the most money: paper it before you build it

Work performed before a signed change order is work you are hoping to get paid for. Once it is installed, your leverage is gone and the conversation becomes a negotiation about whether the owner really approved it. Verbal approvals fail here — not usually because anyone is dishonest, but because two people genuinely remember a hallway conversation differently six weeks later. If a change is urgent and genuinely cannot wait, send a short written note stating the scope and a not-to-exceed price, and get a reply confirming it before the crew starts. An email reply saying "yes, go ahead" is a written record.

The five-minute pricing routine

Same five-part structure as a bid — just smaller. If the five-part estimate structure is new to you, start there.

  1. Quantities. Take off the added work exactly as you would a small job. Do not eyeball it because it's small; small quantities have the largest percentage error.
  2. Materials. Priced from a current quote, plus a realistic waste factor. Small add-on quantities usually carry more waste percentage than the main order, not less — you rarely get to buy a partial unit, and there is no larger order to absorb the offcuts.
  3. Labor. Include the hidden hours: the trip to the supply house, the re-mobilization, the extra cleanup, the time your crew stands around while the change gets decided. On small changes, disruption time frequently exceeds the productive time.
  4. Equipment, disposal, and permits. A dumpster pull, a rental day, a revised permit or an extra inspection. Easy to leave out and impossible to recover later.
  5. Overhead and margin. Apply your normal rates. This is the step small operators skip, and it is the whole reason changes turn a profitable job unprofitable.

Why "just charge my cost" is the expensive option

The instinct on a small add is to charge materials plus a couple of hours and move on — it feels fair, it avoids a difficult conversation, and it seems like goodwill. It is the most reliable way to lose money on a good job.

Your overhead — insurance, truck, phone, software, the hours you spend estimating and invoicing — does not pause because a piece of work was added rather than bid. Every hour on site has to carry its share, and an hour of change-order work has exactly the same claim on your overhead as an hour of contract work. Price a $400 change at cost and you have not done the owner a $60 favor; you have moved $60 from your business to theirs, and then repeated it eleven more times over the course of the job.

The fairness argument runs the other way as well. If a change genuinely saves you money — a deletion, or a substitution to something cheaper — issue the credit at the same rates, promptly and in writing. Consistency in both directions is what makes your pricing defensible when an owner questions it.

Schedule impact belongs in the number

The most commonly missed cost on a change order is time. If added work extends the job by three days, those three days carry general conditions — supervision, site facilities, insurance, and the opportunity cost of the next job you cannot start. Two ways to handle it, and either is fine as long as you pick one and say so:

  • Bundle it. Add the extra days' general conditions into the change order price as a line called "schedule impact — 3 days."
  • State it separately. Price the work, and add a line: "This change extends the completion date by 3 working days." That keeps the price clean and protects you from a late-completion complaint.

What you should not do is stay silent about it. An unstated extension is a promise you did not mean to make.

The one-paragraph change order form

You do not need software for this. Every change order needs seven things, and they fit on half a page:

  1. Job name and change order number. Sequential — CO-01, CO-02. Numbering matters more than it sounds; it is how you prove nothing was missed at final invoicing.
  2. Date.
  3. Description of the change. Plain, specific, and complete. "Remove and replace 40 sq ft of rotted subfloor in the northeast corner of the master bath, including new underlayment" — not "fix floor."
  4. What it does not include. The exclusions line is your protection. If the price covers subfloor but not the finish flooring on top, say so here.
  5. Price, and how it was arrived at. A lump sum is cleanest. If the scope genuinely cannot be defined yet, use time-and-materials with a stated not-to-exceed figure and your hourly rates.
  6. Schedule impact. A number of days, or "none."
  7. Signature lines and a date for both parties. Signed before work begins.

Add one standing line to the bottom of every one: "All other terms of the original contract remain in effect." It keeps a change order from accidentally reopening the whole agreement.

Handling the conversation

Most owners are not trying to get free work. They usually do not know that a small-sounding request carries a supply run, a re-mobilization, and a two-day schedule push. A few habits make the discussion straightforward:

  • Say the price out loud when the request is made — even a rough range — rather than letting it sit unpriced. "I can do that; it'll run somewhere around $600 to $800 and add a day. Want me to write it up?" No one is surprised later.
  • Tell them at contract signing that changes get priced and signed before work starts. A change order is never a surprise if the process was explained on day one.
  • Show the arithmetic when asked. Materials, hours, rate. A price you can break down is a price that stops being argued about.
  • Batch the trivial ones if you want to — but write them down as you go. A running list settled weekly is fine. Nothing settled from memory at the end of the job is fine.
  • Be willing to say no. Some changes are not worth the disruption at any reasonable price. That is a legitimate answer.

The 60-second version

  1. Price it like a mini-bid: quantities, materials, labor, equipment and disposal, then overhead and margin.
  2. Include the hidden hours — supply runs, re-mobilization, cleanup, disruption.
  3. State schedule impact in days, or write "none."
  4. Put it on paper: number it, describe it specifically, say what is excluded, price it, sign it.
  5. Sign it before the work happens — never after.
  6. Issue credits the same way and just as quickly.
