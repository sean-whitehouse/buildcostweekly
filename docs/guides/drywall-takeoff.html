Drywall Takeoff in 15 Minutes

Guides › Estimating · Evergreen — reviewed August 2026

Measure the wall and ceiling square footage, divide by the square footage of one sheet, add 10% waste on a remodel or 5–8% on open new work, and round up to a full sheet. Then size the consumables off that board footage rather than guessing: joint compound, tape, screws, and corner bead all scale predictably. Fifteen minutes with a tape measure and a phone calculator gets you within a few sheets on a typical job.

Drywall is one of the easiest materials to take off and one of the most commonly under-bought. The sheets themselves are rarely the problem — it's the second trip for mud, tape, and bead that eats the afternoon.

Step 1: get the square footage

Two numbers, kept separate:

  • Walls. Perimeter of the room × ceiling height. A 12' × 14' room at 8' ceilings: (12+14+12+14) = 52' perimeter × 8' = 416 SF.
  • Ceilings. Length × width. Same room: 12 × 14 = 168 SF.

Keep them separate because ceilings often take a different product — 5/8" or a sag-resistant board — and because ceiling work carries different labor. That 12×14 bedroom is 584 SF of board total.

Don't deduct small openings

Deduct full-height items — a doorway, a large window wall, a fireplace surround. Leave the small stuff in. A standard window is roughly 15 SF, but the offcuts around it rarely produce a usable piece, so deducting it just moves material off your order and onto your problem list. Most estimators deduct openings over about 25–30 SF and ignore the rest.

Step 2: convert to sheets

Divide total square footage by the coverage of one sheet:

  • 4' × 8' = 32 SF — easiest to handle solo, most joints to finish.
  • 4' × 12' = 48 SF — fewer butt joints, faster finishing, but awkward in tight remodels and stairwells.

Our 584 SF at 4×8: 584 ÷ 32 = 18.25 sheets. Add 10% for a remodel → 20.1 → order 21. Always round up; a partial sheet is not a thing you can buy.

Rule of thumb on sizing: use the longest sheet you can physically get into the room. Every butt joint you eliminate is a joint you don't have to feather, sand, and re-coat, and butt joints are where amateur finishing shows.

Step 3: size the consumables

These are industry rules of thumb, not guarantees — confirm against the label of whatever product your yard actually stocks, since coverage varies by brand and by how heavy a hand your finisher has.

  • Joint compound. Roughly one 4.5-gallon box per 500 SF of board for a standard three-coat finish. Our 584 SF job: two boxes. Level 5 or a skim coat pushes this up sharply.
  • Joint tape. Roughly 400–500 linear feet per 1,000 SF of board. Our job: one 500' roll covers it, and paper tape is cheap enough that a spare roll on the truck is never wasted.
  • Screws. Roughly 1 lb per 100 SF of board (about 300 screws to the pound in 1-1/4" coarse). Our job: 6 lbs, so a 5 lb box plus change, or buy the 25 lb bucket if you hang more than occasionally.
  • Corner bead. Count it — don't estimate it. Walk the plan and tally every outside corner, then multiply by ceiling height. Two outside corners at 8' = 16', so two 8' sticks (or one 10' and cut).

Three places small operators come up short

  1. Ceilings priced as walls. Ceiling board is frequently 5/8", costs more per sheet, and takes materially longer to hang — often cited at roughly 1.5× the labor of the same square footage on a wall. If your bid uses one blended SF rate for everything, ceiling-heavy jobs quietly lose money.
  2. Only counting the first coat of mud. A three-coat finish is tape coat, fill coat, finish coat, and the compound number above assumes all three. Estimating off the tape coat alone gets you about a third of what you need.
  3. Forgetting the non-board line items. Corner bead, control joints, adhesive, sanding screens, and dust protection are small individually and add up to a real number. Put them on the bid template as their own line so they can't be forgotten — see Build a Simple Bid Template That Protects Your Margin.

The 15-minute version

  1. Perimeter × height = wall SF. Length × width = ceiling SF.
  2. Deduct openings over ~25 SF only.
  3. Total SF ÷ 32 (4×8) or ÷ 48 (4×12), + 10% remodel / 5–8% new, round up.
  4. Compound: SF ÷ 500 = boxes. Tape: SF ÷ 1,000 × 450 = linear feet. Screws: SF ÷ 100 = pounds.
  5. Walk the corners and count bead by hand.

Waste percentages are the part people most often get wrong in both directions — for realistic numbers by material, see Waste Factors: The Numbers Pros Actually Use.
