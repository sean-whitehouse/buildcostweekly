How to Read a Lumberyard Quote Line by Line

Guides › Buying materials · Evergreen — reviewed August 2026

Read a lumberyard quote in four passes: check the expiration date at the top, check the unit on every price (per piece, per linear foot, and per thousand board feet are wildly different numbers), check that grade and species match what your plans actually call for, and check what is excluded at the bottom — delivery, offload, tax, and anything marked "subject to change." A quote is a written offer with an expiry, not a price list. Everything you can hold the yard to lives in those four places.

Most small operators treat the quote as one number: the total at the bottom. That total is the least useful thing on the page. It is the sum of a set of assumptions the yard made, and every one of those assumptions is something you either verify now or discover on delivery day.

The header: what this document actually is

Before any line items, the top block tells you whether you have a price or just a conversation.

  • Quote number and date. Both matter for the same reason: when you call to order, this is how the yard finds the pricing you were given. A quote you can't reference by number is a quote you'll re-negotiate at the counter.
  • Expiration / "valid until." The single most important line on the page. Commodity lumber quotes are often held for a short window — sometimes days — while millwork, engineered lumber, and special orders may be held considerably longer. If there is no expiration printed, the quote is generally good for exactly as long as the yard says it is, which is not a thing you want to find out later. Ask for the hold period in writing.
  • Prepared for / job name. Make sure it matches the job. Yards quote different customers different numbers, and a quote made out to someone else is not yours to use.
  • Terms. Net 30, COD, deposit required. This is a cash-flow line, not a price line, but it belongs in your bid thinking — a deposit-required special order changes when you need money, not just how much.

"Quote," "estimate," and "price at time of shipment" are three different promises

A quote is an offer at a stated price for a stated period. An estimate is the yard's best guess and commits them to nothing. "Price in effect at time of shipment" means you are buying at whatever the market does between now and delivery — the yard has explicitly not taken that risk, so you have. All three can arrive on paper that looks identical. Find the words before you assume you have a locked price.

The line items: five columns, three traps

A typical line reads something like: description · quantity · unit · unit price · extended price. The traps are in the middle three.

Trap 1: the unit

The same board can be priced three ways, and mixing them up produces errors big enough to lose a job on:

  • EA / PC (each, per piece). Most common for dimensional lumber at a retail yard. Easy to check: pieces × price.
  • LF (linear foot). Common for trim, siding, and some framing. A 16' stick at $1.10/LF is $17.60 — not $1.10.
  • MBF (per thousand board feet). The wholesale unit, and the one that catches people. This is how the indexes quote lumber, and it is how some yards quote larger framing packages. A $580/MBF price is not comparable to a per-piece price until you convert.

Board feet = (thickness in inches × width in inches × length in feet) ÷ 12, using nominal dimensions. A 2×4×8 is (2 × 4 × 8) ÷ 12 = 5.33 board feet. At $580/MBF that stick is 5.33 ÷ 1,000 × $580 ≈ $3.09 of lumber before the yard's markup, freight, and handling — which is exactly why the index number on our price snapshot never matches the shelf tag. If nominal versus actual sizing is new to you, start with Lumber Grades & Sizes, Explained in Plain English.

Trap 2: the description

"2x6-16" is not a specification. A complete line names species, grade, and treatment, because each one moves the price and the code compliance:

  • Species / species group — SPF, Doug Fir-Larch, Southern Yellow Pine, Hem-Fir. These are not interchangeable; span tables differ by species, so substituting one for another can change what the framing is allowed to carry.
  • Grade — #2 & Better, Select Structural, Stud, Construction. If your plans call for a grade and the quote is for a lower one, the price difference is not a saving.
  • Treatment — untreated, ground-contact, above-ground, fire-retardant. Ground-contact and above-ground pressure-treated look nearly identical on paper and are not the same product for anything touching soil.
  • Engineered products — LVL, LSL, I-joists, and trusses are manufacturer-specific. A quoted "equivalent" from a different manufacturer needs the engineer's or designer's sign-off, not yours.

If a line is vague, ask the yard to re-issue it specific. A yard that will not put the species and grade in writing is telling you something.

Trap 3: the quantity

The yard quoted what you asked for. If your takeoff was short, the quote is short at a very good price. Two habits fix most of this: give the yard your quantities rather than asking them to take off your plans (their takeoff is a courtesy, not a warranty), and confirm whose waste factor is in the number — yours or none at all. Realistic percentages by material are in Waste Factors: The Numbers Pros Actually Use.

The bottom of the page: what is not included

The exclusions block is where a clean-looking quote gets expensive. Check for each of these, and if the quote is silent, ask:

  • Delivery. Included, flat-rate, free over a minimum order, or extra? Free delivery over a threshold quietly changes your ordering strategy.
  • Offload method. Boom truck placement onto a second-story deck is a different service from a curbside drop, and "delivery included" often means the latter. If the crew has to hand-carry a framing package 80 feet, that is labor you didn't bid.
  • Fuel or freight surcharge. Sometimes a separate percentage line, sometimes buried in terms.
  • Sales tax. Frequently excluded from the quoted total. On a five-figure material package this is real money.
  • Restocking fee on returns. Standard on many special orders, and the reason over-ordering isn't free.
  • Special-order lead time. Not a price, but it belongs in your schedule before you commit to a start date.

The four checks before you bid off it

  1. Is it still live? Compare the expiration date to your realistic order date, not today's date. A quote that expires before you'd actually buy is a reference number, not a price.
  2. Do the units line up with your takeoff? Convert everything to the same unit before comparing two yards. Comparing a per-piece quote to an MBF quote by eye is how people pick the more expensive one.
  3. Does the spec match the plans? Species, grade, treatment, and manufacturer on the engineered items. Substitutions are the yard's suggestion and your liability.
  4. What's excluded? Add delivery, offload, surcharge, and tax to the total before it goes anywhere near your bid.

Getting a better quote (without being that customer)

Yards price relationships as much as volume. A few things that reliably help: send a clean quantity list rather than a pile of plans, give a realistic delivery window, ask for the hold period in writing rather than haggling the unit price, and tell them honestly whether you're bidding or buying — most yards will sharpen a number for a job they know is real. When material prices are moving, asking for a written hold that spans your start date costs nothing and is usually granted; whether it's worth paying for is a different question, covered in Lock In Material Prices or Float Them?.

The 60-second version

  1. Top of page: find the expiration date and the word "quote" (not "estimate," not "price at time of shipment").
  2. Every line: confirm the unit — EA, LF, or MBF — before you read the price.
  3. Every line: confirm species, grade, and treatment against the plans.
  4. Bottom of page: add delivery, offload, surcharges, and tax.
  5. Then, and only then, use the total in your bid.
