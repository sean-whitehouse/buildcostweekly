What this means for your bid

Direction: the two sides of this market just swapped roles. The framing composite — the cash index your lumberyard's counter price actually tracks — came in at $536 per thousand board feet for the week ending August 14, down 3.9% ($22) in a single week and down 2.2% on the month. Madison's notes it is the first weekly decline since May. Meanwhile lumber futures, which had been falling for a month, ticked back up: $581 on August 19, about 2.3% above where we found them on August 14, though still 10.1% below a month ago.

Why that matters more than a normal week. The last two snapshots said the same thing: futures were falling, the cash market wasn't, and you should not shave a bid off a decline that had not reached your yard. That reservation has now partly cleared. The wholesale cash market did move, and it moved down hard enough in one week to be worth knowing about. This is the first evidence in this cycle that the softness is reaching the side of the market you buy from.

Two cautions, because one week is one week. First, a single print after three months of increases is a data point, not a trend — the composite could just as easily print flat or higher next week. Second, the cash index is still wholesale. Your yard's counter price typically lags these moves by two to six weeks, and a yard sitting on inventory bought at $558 has no obligation to hand the difference back. The move is a reason to ask, not a discount you already have.

Still on the calendar: the Commerce Department's final results in the seventh administrative review of Canadian softwood lumber duties. The preliminary combined antidumping and countervailing rate was 24.83%, down from the 35.16% currently in effect. The final was expected as early as August but can be extended into October, so it may not land this month at all. A final number can come in above or below the preliminary one, and the cash market is the side that would feel it.

One honesty note on the table: the composite's year-over-year figure reads 0.0% — unchanged from a year ago — even though the price just fell 3.9%. That is the comparison base moving, not the market going quiet. Lumber was also sliding through mid-August last year, so the year-ago benchmark dropped alongside it. Same caveat on the futures year column, which improved from −6.8% to −2.5%. Read the level and the month change; the year column is noisier than it looks.

What that means practically: for the first time in a month the guidance below actually changes — modestly, and in one direction: it is now worth re-pricing rather than assuming your last quote still holds.

  • Bidding a job that starts within 30 days? Get a fresh written quote before you bid, especially if the one on your desk is more than two or three weeks old. The cash market moved nearly 4% in a week, and a quote written before August 14 was priced off a different market. That is not the same as assuming you will get 4% back — it is a reason to make the call rather than reuse the number.
  • Bidding now for a start 60+ days out? Paying a premium to lock a price is a weaker argument this week than last, not a stronger one — you generally do not pay up to lock in a market that is moving your way. The duty review is still outstanding and can cut either way, which is a good reason to ask your yard for a free written hold that spans your start date. The ask costs nothing and takes the outcome off your plate.
  • Buying for a job already under contract? Still no price case for buying early — if anything the case is weaker now, since the recent direction has been down. If you have a delivery you genuinely cannot let slip, buying ahead is a scheduling decision. Make it on those grounds and be honest with yourself that it is not a price call.

Note on consistency: last snapshot said that if the next composite reading came in below $558, the guidance would change — that it would be the first real evidence of relief reaching the counter. It came in at $536. So the guidance changed, as promised: from "do not bid a decline that hasn't reached your yard" to "the wholesale side moved, so re-quote before you bid." What has not changed is the underlying rule — you bid off the written quote in your hand, not off this table.

FAQ

How often are these prices updated?

Every week. The date at the top of the page ("Week of...") is the week this snapshot covers.

Where do these numbers come from?

Two sources, both linked in the Sources section above: Trading Economics for lumber futures, and the NAHB/Madison's Lumber Price Index for the framing lumber composite. Both are dated so you can check the primary source yourself.

Why do the futures price and the composite price sometimes move in different directions?

Futures are traders' forward bet on where lumber is headed; the Madison's composite tracks what's actually trading in the cash market right now. They usually move together but can diverge for weeks at a time — when they disagree, the "What this means for your bid" callout says so.

Will my local lumberyard price match this exactly?

No. These are wholesale indexes, not retail prices — your yard's counter price will differ and typically lags these moves by 2–6 weeks. Always confirm with a written supplier quote before you bid; don't bid off the index number.

What materials besides lumber are tracked?

Right now, just framing lumber (futures and the Madison's composite). OSB/panel, drywall, rebar/steel, and ready-mix concrete are being added as reliable free sources are verified — see the note above if there's one you want prioritized.
