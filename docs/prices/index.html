What this means for your bid

Direction: both sides down, and now moving together. The framing composite — the cash index your lumberyard's counter price actually tracks — came in at $530 per thousand board feet for the week ending August 21, down $6 (1.1%) on the week and down $24 (4.3%) from $554 a month ago. That is the second consecutive weekly decline. Lumber futures gave back last week's bounce: $575 on August 24, about 1.1% below the $581 in the last snapshot, and 11.3% under where they sat a month ago.

Why the second print matters. Last week's snapshot flagged the composite's first fall since May and called it what it was — one data point, not a trend. A second decline is not proof of a trend either, but it does remove the most likely innocent explanation: that the first drop was a one-week correction to an overshoot. Two prints in a row, in the same direction, alongside futures that are 11% below last month, is a market that is softening rather than one that blipped.

What is doing the softening: demand, not supply. The Census Bureau put July housing starts at a 1,239,000 annual rate — down 12.4% from June and 13.5% from a year ago, with single-family down 9.9%. Mortgage rates near one-year highs are the usual explanation. Fewer houses getting framed means less lumber getting bought. That matters for how you read this: a decline driven by weak demand tends to persist longer than one driven by a temporary supply glut, but it is also the kind of decline that reverses quickly if rates move. Nobody knows which way rates go, this page included.

The standing caution has not changed. The cash index is wholesale. Your yard's counter price typically lags these moves by two to six weeks, and a yard sitting on inventory bought at $554 has no obligation to hand the difference back. Two weeks of decline is a reason to ask, not a discount you already have.

Still on the calendar: the Commerce Department's final results in the seventh administrative review of Canadian softwood lumber duties. The preliminary combined antidumping and countervailing rate was 24.83%, down from the 35.16% currently in effect. The final was expected as early as August but can be extended into October; as of today it has not been published. A final number can come in above or below the preliminary one, and the cash market is the side that would feel it.

One honesty note on the table, same as last week: the composite's year-over-year figure reads flat — roughly unchanged from a year ago — even though the price has now fallen two weeks running. That is the comparison base moving, not the market going quiet. Lumber was also sliding through late August last year, so the year-ago benchmark dropped alongside it. Read the level and the month change; the year column is noisier than it looks.

What that means practically: the guidance below is the same as last week's, held with a bit more confidence. Re-price rather than assume your last quote still holds.

  • Bidding a job that starts within 30 days? Get a fresh written quote before you bid, especially if the one on your desk is more than two or three weeks old. The cash market is down 4.3% on the month, and a quote written before mid-August was priced off a different market. That is not the same as assuming you will get 4.3% back — it is a reason to make the call rather than reuse the number.
  • Bidding now for a start 60+ days out? Paying a premium to lock a price remains the weaker argument — you generally do not pay up to lock in a market that has moved your way two weeks running. The duty review is still outstanding and can cut either way, which is a good reason to ask your yard for a free written hold that spans your start date. The ask costs nothing and takes the outcome off your plate.
  • Buying for a job already under contract? Still no price case for buying early — the recent direction has been down. If you have a delivery you genuinely cannot let slip, buying ahead is a scheduling decision. Make it on those grounds and be honest with yourself that it is not a price call.

Note on consistency: last snapshot changed the guidance from "do not bid a decline that hasn't reached your yard" to "the wholesale side moved, so re-quote before you bid," and said one week is one week. The second week came in lower, so that guidance stands rather than reverting. If the composite prints flat or higher next week, this page will say so plainly instead of quietly dropping the subject. What does not change either way is the underlying rule — you bid off the written quote in your hand, not off this table.

FAQ

How often are these prices updated?

Every week. The date at the top of the page ("Week of...") is the week this snapshot covers.

Where do these numbers come from?

Two sources, both linked in the Sources section above: Trading Economics for lumber futures, and the NAHB/Madison's Lumber Price Index for the framing lumber composite. Both are dated so you can check the primary source yourself.

Why do the futures price and the composite price sometimes move in different directions?

Futures are traders' forward bet on where lumber is headed; the Madison's composite tracks what's actually trading in the cash market right now. They usually move together but can diverge for weeks at a time — when they disagree, the "What this means for your bid" callout says so.

Will my local lumberyard price match this exactly?

No. These are wholesale indexes, not retail prices — your yard's counter price will differ and typically lags these moves by 2–6 weeks. Always confirm with a written supplier quote before you bid; don't bid off the index number.

What materials besides lumber are tracked?

Right now, just framing lumber (futures and the Madison's composite). OSB/panel, drywall, rebar/steel, and ready-mix concrete are being added as reliable free sources are verified — see the note above if there's one you want prioritized.
